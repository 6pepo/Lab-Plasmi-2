	\section{PMT and scintillator crystal}
The operation of a scintillator crystal is based on the process of radiation-matter interaction and its purpose is to transform the signal given by a photon into a measurable electrical signal. Following the interaction with an incident photon, an electron of the crystal is released, losing energy and exciting the electrons of the semiconductor, taking them from the valence band to the conduction band. 

These, de-excited, emit an optical photon that cannot be absorbed by the semiconductor because it is doped. This photon produces, by photoelectric effect on the photocathode, an electron that is amplified inside a photomultiplier by a series of dynodes placed at different potentials, guaranteeing a gain of $10^{5}$ or $10^{6}$ electrons.

In our case, a crystal of Lanthanum Bromide doped at $5\%$ with Cerium was used. Lanthanum Bromide contains a radioactive isotope, $^{138}\ch{La}$ (a.i $0.08881\%$) with a half-life of $1.03  \cdot 10^{11}$ years that decays by electron capture (BR $65.2\%$) on an excited state of $^{138}\ch{Ba}$. When de-excited, it emits a $\gamma$ beam at 1436 KeV. 

The choice of this crystal was dictated precisely by its intrinsic radioactivity which allows to obtain important information on the detection efficiency of the instrument and allows to carry out its calibration. Furthermore, thanks to the short scintillation time, the crystal allows to have a high counting rate similar to what occurs in plasmas.

\chapter{Data Analysis}
First, connect the PMT to a high-voltage generator and, since you want to polarize the photo-tube negatively, it must be connected to the negative channel of the generator, while the positive channel of the latter is zeroed via a coaxial cable (which functions as ground).

The photo-tube is then connected to an oscilloscope and, by observing how the output signal varies, the voltage to use is determined. Even at low voltages, a signal begins to appear on the oscilloscope. This is because $^{138}\ch{La}$ isotope, being radioactive, emits radiation, which, once converted into "visible" photons by the scintillator crystal, is detected by the PMT. After choosing the potential difference, equal to 1000 V.

The following are the results obtained for the centroids of the two Gaussians and the cumulative one:
\begin{itemize}
\item Peak - 1440 keV: (1120.98 $\pm$ 14.99) Ch
\item Peak - 1472 keV: (1133.25 $\pm$ 12.46) Ch
\item Cumulative peak: (1129.69 $\pm$ 16.43) Ch
\end{itemize}

At this point, the energy resolution of the detector was estimated:

\begin{equation}
R=\frac{FWHM}{H_0}=\frac{2.35\sqrt{FH_0}}{H_0}
\end{equation}

where FWHM is the width at half-height of the peak and H$_{0}$ is its centroid. Taking the centroid of the cumulative Gaussian and its area as a reference.

As previously indicated, we assume that the cumulative peak is the centroid, thus calculating the resolution R of the peak in the Poissonian limit we obtain: 6.99$\pm$0.05 \%, instead of given resolution equal to: 3\%. This leads us to introduce the Fano factor that is $\approx$18.40$\pm$0.26\%.

		\subsection{Intrinsic radioactivity and Detection efficiency}
The objective is to estimate the absolute intrinsic radiation efficiency of lanthanum, that is, the number of pulses the detector is capable of recording compared to the number of pulses emitted by the source. The procedure is divided into three main steps:
\begin{itemize}
\item crystal volume estimation ($V_{crystal}$, equation \ref{volume})
\item source activity estimation ($A_{\ch{LaBr3}}$, equation \ref{attivita})
\item estimation of detection efficiency for intrinsic radioactivity ($\epsilon_{abs, RAD.INTR.}$, equation \ref{eff_int})
\end{itemize}

Knowing the nominal efficiency of the instrument, it is possible to estimate the volume of the crystal as the sum of the mass fractions of the various components. Therefore, having a crystal of $\ch{La}(:\ch{Ce}\,5\%)\ch{Br3}$, we can estimate the volume according to the equation:

\begin{equation}
 \begin{array}{ll}
V_{crystal} & =\frac{m_{\ch{LaBr3}}}{\rho_{\ch{LaBr3}}}+\frac{m_{\ch{CeBr3}}}{\rho_{\ch{CeBr3}}}=\\
\null & =\left(\frac{\tau_{1/2}}{ln(2)t}\frac{\#_{\ch{LaBr3}}}{BR\epsilon AI}\frac{1}{N_{A}}\right)\left(\frac{mm_{\ch{LaBr3}}}{\rho_{\ch{LaBr3}}}95\%+\frac{mm_{\ch{CeBr3}}}{\rho_{\ch{CeBr3}}}5\%\right) =\\
\null & = 423.07 \pm 67.08 cm^{3}
\end{array}
\label{volume}
 \end {equation}

The constants and parameter in the formulas are reported in appendix \ref{parameter}.\\
The intrinsic activity $A_{\ch{LaBr3}}$ is defined as the product of the number of active \ch{La} atoms and the decay constant $\lambda$:
 
\begin{equation}
\begin{array}{ll}
A_{\ch{LaBr3}} & =N_{AA}\lambda=\\
\null & =N_A\frac{(\rho_{\ch{LaBr3}}95\%+\rho_{\ch{CeBr3}}5\%)V_{crystal}}{(mm_{\ch{LaBr3}}95\%+mm_{\ch{CeBr3}}5\%)}AI\frac{ln(2)}{\tau_{1/2}} =\\
\null & = 651.88 \pm 103.15 Bq
\end{array}
\label{attivita}
\end {equation}

It remains only to estimate the intrinsic absolute efficiency of the detector:

\begin{equation}
\epsilon_{abs, RAD.INTR.}=\frac{\#_{\ch{LaBr3}}}{A_{\ch{LaBr3}}\delta tBR} = 18.9870\pm0.0002\%
\label{eff_int}
\end {equation}

where $\#_{\ch{LaBr3}}$ represents the area of the electron capture peak, that is the number of photons recorded by the detector, $\delta t$ is the measurement time interval, and BR is the acronym for Branching Ratio, which represents the probability with which $^{138}\ch{La}$ decays by electron capture (the remaining percentage is due to decays $\beta^{-}$).

After calculating the absolute efficiency of the detector relative to the intrinsic radioactivity of the crystal, through a Monte Carlo simulation, we find a relationship between this constant and the efficiency relative to a cesium source. \begin{equation}
\epsilon_{ratio}=\frac{\epsilon_{abs,\ch{Cs}}}{\epsilon_{abs, RAD.INTR.}}

 		\subsection{$^{137}\ch{Cs}$ measures}
With the data provided it is therefore possible to estimate the activity of the cesium source by analyzing the counts detected in the appendix \ref{app_cesio} and applying the following formula we get the figure \ref{acti}.
\begin{equation}
A_{\ch{Cs}}=\frac{\#_{\ch{Cs}}}{\epsilon_{abs,\ch{Cs}}\delta t}=\frac{\#_{\ch{Cs}}}{\epsilon_{ratio}\epsilon_{abs, RAD.INTR.}\delta t}
\end {equation}

 \begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{Attivita}
\caption{Activity}
\label{acti}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Expected activity}: Knowing the initial activity of \ch{^{137}Cs} of approximately 41.3 kBq with a half-life of 30.08 years (as reported in the appendix \ref{parameter}) the expected activity is estimated using the radioactive decay formula
 
\begin{equation}
A_{\ch{Cs}}=A_{0}e^{-\lambda t}=A_{0}e^{-\frac{ln(2)}{\tau_{1/2}}\delta t} = 29.23\pm0.1 kBq
\end {equation}

we can therefore assume that the data provided for geometric efficiency $\epsilon_{ratio}$ are not complete.

\chapter{Error propagation}
\begin{equation}
\sigma^{2}_{F}=\sum^{N}_{i=1}\left(\frac{\delta F}{\delta x_{i}}\right)^{2}\sigma^{2}_{i} + 2\sum_{i<j}\frac{\delta F}{\delta x_{i}}\frac{\delta F}{\delta x_{j}}\rho_{ij}\sigma_{i}\sigma{j}
\end{equation}
$\sigma_{i}$ represents the measurement error for the i-quantity, while $\rho_{ij}$ is the correlation coefficient.
